Demotopia

Democratic Utopia

The city has become safer, clearer and fairer. The system no longer sees a single act in isolation; it sees the whole person — routes, relationships, choices, obligations and silences.

But what remains of freedom when society can prove that it knows a person more accurately than that person knows themselves?

Chapter 1. The machine never arrived

The system already sees a completed structure. The investigator is trying to establish where a person’s proven involvement ends and knowledge attributed to them begins.

Open excerptClose excerpt

The investigator opened the detention file for the ninth address.

The team entered at 05:40:08.

There was no physical resistance.

The personal node was removed without damage.

The protective circuit was restored one minute and forty-two seconds later.

A power of attorney, company registration records and several devices were seized.

He stopped the recording before the detainee himself appeared.

For the moment, there was no need to see the man.

The file already contained everything required for his function within the case.

“Do we have confirmation that he knew the end user?” the investigator asked.

The analyst did not answer at once. Not because she doubted, but because she was checking which kind of answer the system permitted.

“No direct message has been found.”

“The technical purpose?”

“No direct message has been found.”

“The altered configuration?”

“No direct message has been found.”

The team leader turned his head.

“Then what has been found?”

The analyst displayed a list.

A meeting with Dmitry.

A power of attorney issued to a person who had contact with two internal participants.

An expected fee.

A company without which the legal chain could not operate.

No request concerning the end user.

No request concerning the technical specification.

No review of the representative’s actions after the power of attorney was issued.

“The last three are not messages,” the investigator said.

“No. They are behavioural absences.”

“The absence of an action is not knowledge.”

“Not by itself.”

“Keep them in a separate category.”

“They already are.”

On the map they were marked not with lines, but with empty circles.

The system was making no claim.

It was showing the places where a person could have asked and did not.

The team leader walked slowly along the wall.

“He did not need to understand the machine’s technical construction to know that he was providing a company for an unusual transaction.”

“No,” the investigator agreed.

“And he did not need to receive a letter saying, ‘the end user is a military plant,’ in order to recognise the risk.”

“No.”

“Then what question are you asking?”

The investigator enlarged the fourth personal connection — the journey with Dmitry.

“Whether he knew it was an unusual transaction.”

“Or chose not to know?”

“That is the next question.”

The team leader looked at him without irritation.

“Good. First establish what he knew. Then establish why he did not learn the rest.”

It was the correct sequence.

That was why the investigator trusted the person leading the group.

A recording from Dmitry’s apartment opened on the central wall.

Not an image. Only sound and a precise timeline.

05:47:11 — a digital-unit officer says the surname of the ninth detainee.

05:47:12 — Dmitry inhales.

05:47:13 — the restraints move.

05:47:14 — a voice:

“Why him? He isn’t one of us…”

The service layer proposed three possible semantic tags:

spontaneous distancing;

an attempt to exclude a participant from the inner circle;

the tactical construction of a defensive account.

Each tag had a probability attached.

The investigator removed the probabilities from the working display.

“Why?” the analyst asked.

“They create the impression that we have measured meaning.”

“We measured correspondence with known behavioural models.”

“Exactly.”

He kept the three tags, but reordered them alphabetically.

The team leader watched him.

“Did you believe him?”

“No.”

“Then what troubles you?”

“He did not ask ‘who is that?’ He asked ‘why him?’”

“He had seen the photograph and the surname.”

“Which is why he did not need to ask who.”

“Then he knew him.”

“He knew the person. Not necessarily the person’s role.”

The team leader moved closer to the panel and listened to the phrase again.

Dmitry spoke calmly, but not as he had before. For the first time there was something in his voice that had not yet become a decision.

Surprise.

Possibly genuine.

Possibly professionally genuine.

“People at his level understand very quickly which account benefits them,” the team leader said.

“Agreed.”

“Sometimes before everyone else has realised that an account has appeared at all.”

“Agreed.”

“Then we leave it as an unverified statement.”

“Yes.”

A note appeared on the timeline:

Initial remark. Evidentiary value undetermined. Verify whether the subject belonged to the inner circle of knowledge.

It was more precise than “defending him.”

And more precise than “excluding him.”

The general map returned to the wall.

The ninth address reappeared between two parts of the chain.

The elegant word “between” did not mean peripheral. A bridge stands between two banks, yet all movement passes across it.

“You are still separating him from the structure.”

The investigator looked at the map.

“No. I am separating the structure from what was inside his head.”

The room became slightly quieter.

Work continued; personal filters opened a narrow zone of privacy around their conversation.

The team leader did not take the words as an objection.

“Good. But until we know what was in his head, society must still be protected from what was done in his name.”

About the book

for the reader

Demotopia does not begin with catastrophe, open dictatorship or a ruined city. It begins in a clean, organised and desirable near-future Warsaw — a place where the public system can prevent errors, distribute risk and make decisions from an almost complete picture.

The problem begins when that picture becomes more precise than a person’s understanding of themselves. The system may neither lie nor persecute, and it may never violate its own rules. It may act lawfully, transparently and with the approval of the majority — while still depriving a person of the right to remain the final author of their own identity.

Democracy does not abolish coercion or remove dictatorship as a possibility. It distributes power across people, rules, laws, votes, expertise, procedures and technical systems. No single tyrant can be identified: every participant may act reasonably and in good faith, while their combined decision becomes a power that is almost impossible to challenge.

Freedom in Demotopia is therefore not treated as the familiar conflict between an individual and the state. The person is confronted not by another person’s whim, but by a shared picture assembled from their own actions and accepted by society as more reliable than their testimony about themselves. The system does not need a confession. It already has facts, rules for evaluating them, a public mandate and the authority to decide.

Dictatorship does not begin only where rules disappear. It may also begin where rules become so complete that a person has nowhere left to exist outside their final interpretation.

Without spoilers

what the reader will encounter

A Warsaw that works

The city does not look like a prison. It is convenient, clean, safe and logical. That very persuasiveness makes it unsettling.

The person as an aggregate

Routes, contracts, relationships, habits, financial traces and omissions form a figure that nobody deliberately designed, yet which produces real consequences.

Power without an evil face

There is no single tyrant. Power is distributed among the majority, the law, expertise, interfaces and conscientious officials.

Responsibility without intention

The novel asks whether a person is responsible for the image and function produced by their actions when they never conceived those actions as a single plan.

Artistic-research framework

literature as a laboratory

Demotopia is neither a technological forecast nor a political pamphlet. It is an artistic inquiry into how democracy changes when society gains the ability to assemble an almost complete picture of a person, their decisions and the consequences of those decisions.

The novel treats democracy not as the opposite of dictatorship, but as a complex form of distributed power. Majorities, laws, courts, experts, digital systems and procedures of mutual oversight can restrain the arbitrary will of a single ruler. Yet the same distribution can conceal coercion itself: no one makes the final decision alone, but the decision exists, acts and acquires the moral authority of collective agreement.

Fiction can examine what a proposition alone cannot contain. Each chapter places a person inside a concrete situation in which the system acts carefully, lawfully and often justly. The reader sees not an abstract mechanism, but a sequence of evidence, doubt, correction and decision — and must decide where protection of society becomes appropriation of the person.

The central question is not whether the system makes mistakes. A mistake can be found, appealed and corrected. The more difficult case is one in which the system is factually right, careful in its language and open to revision — and still creates a whole person-image that becomes stronger than the person’s own account.

Demotopia examines not democracy collapsing into a caricature of tyranny, but an impersonal dictatorship emerging from conscientious rule-following, public agreement and technically impeccable knowledge.

Core themes

the novel’s central nodes

Freedom

Not only the ability to choose among available options, but the right to remain the author of one’s own meaning and to challenge the finality of an external description.

Data

Not a neutral archive, but the material from which society constructs a legal, social and moral figure of a person.

Democracy

A system grounded in participation, rules and safety that can gradually turn completeness of knowledge into a supreme form of control.

Responsibility

The boundary between what a person did, what they meant to do, and what emerged from many actions independently of intention.

Safety

A genuine public good that can become an argument against human unpredictability, private error and the right to take risks.

Identity

Whether a person retains the right to define themselves when the external picture is more demonstrable and persuasive than the internal one.

Who it is for

a reader’s point of entry

The novel may speak to readers of philosophical fiction, social science fiction, legal drama and dystopias without decorative brutality — readers interested not only in control, but in the seduction of a system that genuinely works, prevents threats and can explain every action it takes.

Demotopia requires no technical or legal background. It asks only for a willingness to hold two truths at once: society has the right to protect itself, and a person has the right not to be exhausted by society’s final description of them.
