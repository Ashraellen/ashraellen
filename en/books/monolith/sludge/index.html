Chapter 6. First Steps through Sludge.

§ 6.2. Warehouses for the Preservation of Shards

For breakfast they were given porridge. Not food in the human sense, but precisely porridge: grey, viscous, with the characteristic taste that appears when grain has spent too long in a damp sack and is then boiled not to satisfy hunger, but to fulfil a quota. It was poured into identical metal bowls. The steam carried no aroma, only a warm reminder that biology had not yet been entirely abolished. People ate in silence, quickly, without pleasure and without disgust. Adam ate too—not even from hunger, but because his body had already begun to act within the limits of what was provided. The spoon scraped against the metal, and that sound seemed almost domestic beside everything surrounding them.

After breakfast, Segment No. 404 was formed into ranks and driven towards the hangars.

They stretched along the main unloading line: long, concrete, low, with dark seams and warped gates. In places the walls had peeled away, revealing rusty reinforcement. On the signs, instead of names, there were half-erased inscriptions, and above one entrance Adam managed to read the official designation:

“WAREHOUSES FOR THE PRESERVATION OF SHARDS.”

The name was so exact that for a moment it did not feel like an official formula. Only later did Adam realise that here, as everywhere in MONOLITH, the word “preservation” did not mean care, and “shards” did not mean objects. Everything that the system had already rejected but could still use was stored here.

Grey-haired, walking beside him, gave a short snort when he noticed Adam reading the sign.

“You like it?” he asked. “A good place. Old imperial warehouses. Everything those who thought themselves smarter than the system failed to remove is rotting here. Then MONOLITH arrives and calls it a resource again.”

Inside the hangar it was gloomy, damp and smelled of old iron, mould, machine oil and that peculiar warehouse dust that does not simply lie on things but enters them, changes their temperature, weight and sound. Along the walls stood racks crammed with yellowed boxes, sealed crates, bundles of straps, rusty brackets, broken helmets and incomplete sets of something that had once had an exact purpose but now existed only as the remainder of suitability.

“Approach one at a time!” Ork barked, and his voice travelled lightly through the hangar as if through an empty reservoir. “Receive your Integration Instruments and means of protection. Resistance to Ego is to remain with personnel!”

The last phrase made Kazik give a short grin. Giena, it seemed, did not hear it at all—he was already counting the columns of issue numbers on the wall and trying to calculate which queue moved faster.

Adam was called among the first.

The storekeeper pushed an Instrument of Argumentation into his hands.

It was heavier than it looked. Not the pleasant weight of a reliable mechanism, but the awkward, dead heaviness of a thing assembled from parts that had not been intended to work together. The metal was scratched, the grip worn smooth by someone else’s palm and the bolt moved with a dry rasp. It smelled of preservative grease, damp cloth and old use.

“You’ll get ammunition if you make it to the Contact Zone,” the storekeeper said without looking at him. “For now, learn to carry it.”

Beside the weapon, Adam was issued a protective vest.

It was specifically issued, not fitted. Nobody asked his size or checked the straps. The vest was simply pushed across the counter together with the rest, as if protection were not meant to fit a person, but merely to be recorded beside their name.

It looked heavier than it felt. Adam lifted it, expecting resistance, but the body of the vest yielded almost too easily.

His memory, trained by old standards, immediately supplied the expected weight, rigidity and density. This vest matched none of them. Its fabric was thick and its outline convincing, but inside there was a strange emptiness.

“Look,” Grey-haired said, turning the vest over in his hands. “At least they took care of us. A thick one. Probably the latest development.”

Then he stopped.

At first he only stared at one of the seams.

Then he began to laugh. Quietly, almost soundlessly, bending lower and lower as if the joke were too heavy to hold upright.

“They took care of us?” Kazik asked. “What is it?”

Grey-haired came closer and pulled a short improvised knife from his sleeve.

He cut through the seam of the vest.

Something dry and flat slipped out from under the fabric.

It fell at Adam’s feet, straight into the sludge carried in from outside on their boots.

It was an ordinary pine board.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Kazik stared at the board; Giena looked from it to his own vest, as if the object on the floor had suddenly changed the weight of everything they were wearing.

Grey-haired picked the board up, weighed it in his hand and shoved it back towards the torn opening.

“Outside—a hero of the Pride Protocol,” he said. “Inside—an empty cavity covered over with firewood.”

The board slid from his fingers and sank edge-first into the dark muck.

Adam was struck not by the deception itself, but by its simplicity. Nobody had even tried to imitate armour properly. The vest only had to preserve the outline of protection long enough for the issue to be signed off.

“How are we supposed to go to Contact with this?” he asked.

Grey-haired had already returned to examining the weapon, as though the board had merely confirmed a conclusion he had reached long before.

“Don’t whine,” he said. “If you want to live, write to your mother in the Lower Sector. Let her sell the little summer house, take out a loan with the Department of Balance and send you a proper plate through a SpecTransPort courier. Everything is simple here: the system does not give you a resource. It makes you and your relatives pay for your own disposal. That is the highest form of enslavement—when you buy your own coffin so MONOLITH does not have to spend money on your death.”

His words sounded dry, almost bureaucratic, as if he were reciting not a complaint but the operating instructions for a familiar mechanism.

Kazik and Curly were already putting on their shells, no longer looking inside. Their movements had become quicker and more careful, as though ignorance itself might still count as a layer of protection.

Giena felt through his vest for a long time, found something hard and exhaled with relief.

“Mine is wood too,” he said a moment later. “Only thinner.”

“Then statistically you’ll die cheaper,” Grey-haired answered.

Adam looked at the empty internal volume of his vest—the space where there should have been a plate, but where only the trace of its outline remained.

It was a pure mechanism of substitution: outside, function; inside, imitation.

The contour resembled transport, the warehouse resembled a preservation cell, the vest resembled protection and life resembled the continuation of life. The system did not need the substance of things. It needed their form to remain convincing until the next signature.

Outside the hangar, the enormous sorting station was already grinding into motion. Conveyors groaned, iron struck iron and names dissolved into numbers beneath the loudspeakers.

Giena checked the terminal and frowned: beside Segment 404, a new destination had appeared—Cobweb. He signed beneath it automatically, still trying to calculate whether the route shortened their waiting time or merely changed the manner of disposal.

Grey-haired noticed his calculations.

“Don’t calculate,” he said. “The system doesn’t give you a resource. It makes you pay for your own disposal.”

Adam looked up.

“What market are we entering?”

Grey-haired grinned.

“The market of last illusions. You will see: they will sell you connection, meaning, warmth, hope and the semblance of clean fabric for a second-hand report. Everything at the price of your fear.”

The gate opened, revealing the next stage.

The board remained in the sludge behind them.

A pine plate—the material substance of the deception, too crude to be called technology and too deliberate to be called an accident.

And that was worse than any words.

About the book

the second stage of MONOLITH

SLUDGE begins after the first crack. The wall loses its rigidity, but freedom does not arrive: pressure becomes an environment, softens boundaries and makes participation more habitual than resistance.

At the centre is Adam, who has lost his work, home and former supports. His personal fall proves to be part of a process in which the human self is converted into a resource and material of the environment.

This is Volume II of MONOLITH: the viscous zone between solidity and decompression, through which nobody can pass clean.

Without spoilers

what the reader will encounter

Adam loses his work, home and previous support. His life does not collapse in a single blow; it slowly comes apart at the seams.

Temporary spaces, other people’s rules, hunger, fatigue, ritual and false calm turn separate concessions into a change in the composition of the person.

In SLUDGE, violence works through habituation. A person is not broken at once; they are led towards a state in which disagreement becomes too heavy.

Artistic-research frame

environment, fatigue, loss of boundaries

SLUDGE studies pressure that has become an environment. Resistance is not destroyed at once—it is spent through fatigue, hunger, dependence, repetition and false care.

Viscosity creates an illusion of movement: a person still chooses, but the choice already takes place inside an environment that has altered their boundaries.

The book’s main object is the moment when participation precedes consent and the environment begins to speak in the person’s voice.

For whom

reader entry

For readers of dystopia and psychological thrillers who care not only about open conflict with a system, but also about the quieter process: fatigue, habituation, dissolving boundaries and participation in what recently seemed impossible.

For those interested in stories without convenient heroics or a clear line between external violence and internal consent. SLUDGE works where a person still considers themselves separate although the environment has already changed their composition.

Place in the trilogy

MONOLITH / Volume II

SLUDGE is Volume II of MONOLITH and the stage of viscous deformation. BETON establishes the wall and first crack; SLUDGE shows how pressure then becomes an environment, resistance tires and complicity enters habit.

This is not liberation after the collapse of solid order. SLUDGE lies between BETON and GAS: former boundaries no longer protect, but the complete decompression of form has not yet arrived.

What must not be confused

boundaries of the book

SLUDGE is not a story of liberation after the wall collapses. The loss of solid form does not mean freedom: control can move closer when it becomes an environment.

It is not only a story of personal downfall and not an instruction manual for resistance. The book studies a logic that works through vulnerability, fatigue, necessity, ritual, habituation and gradual participation.

SLUDGE is not a political pamphlet or cyberpunk about technology. It is the second volume of an artistic investigation into pressure that changes its state of matter and begins to act as the substance of everyday life.
